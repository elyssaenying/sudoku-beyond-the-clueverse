Can you judge a Sudoku by its clues?

Short answer

No—not reliably.

Clue count is almost unrelated to the supplied solver difficulty. A product using it as the main labelling rule would give players inconsistent expectations.

The personal starting point

A player’s hunch became a product question.

After years of playing Sudoku, I had picked up enough techniques and tricks to know that fewer clues did not always mean a harder puzzle. I suspected that where the clues were placed mattered more than how many there were. But when friends found puzzles I considered relatively easy just as difficult as the others, I began to question what “difficulty” actually meant, and whether it came from the puzzle, the player or both.

A digital Sudoku product may need to classify thousands—or millions—of boards. If it uses an intuitive shortcut that does not match solving effort, “Easy” and “Hard” become unreliable promises to the player.

The result that broke the shortcut 23 clues

Puzzles with exactly 23 clues range from a 0.0 solver rating to the dataset maximum of 8.5.

Same clue count. Very different solver ratings.

What the analysis found

Three pieces of evidence changed the decision.

01

Association

More clues do not create a dependable difficulty ladder.

Pearson correlation between clue count and rating is 0.035; Spearman correlation is 0.041. Both sit well below the project’s 0.10 threshold for a practically useful relationship.

Prediction

Visible structure still does not predict the rating.

The clue-count model explains 0.12% of test-set variation. A separate linear model using clue distribution, basic location and symmetry features explains only 0.69%. Neither contains enough signal for dependable classification.

My interpretation

What surprised me was not the clue count.

It was within my expectations that clue count would not align closely with difficulty. What surprised me was how little our basic summaries of clue location, balance and symmetry explained: only 0.69% of the variation in solver ratings. It made me think that difficulty may depend less on how a puzzle looks and more on the techniques and reasoning required to solve it.

That experience can differ from player to player. Familiarity with solving techniques, the amount of help needed and years of practice all shape which patterns someone can recognise. Sudoku now feels a little like chess to me: the more you play, the more you begin to see patterns that were invisible when you started.

Product recommendation

Let real player behaviour define difficulty.

Use solver complexity to give new puzzles a provisional difficulty tier, but let observed player behaviour become the primary evidence once enough data is available. Measure completion time, hints, mistakes, restarts and abandonment separately across demonstrated skill levels, then provide skill-calibrated labels. If one catalogue-wide label is required, combine the group-level results using transparent weights that reflect the product’s player base.

Solver estimate→Player calibration→Skill-level label

How the conclusion was reached

Built to withstand the obvious questions.

The analysis separates confirmatory tests from exploration and keeps the boundary between solver difficulty and human experience explicit.

  1. 01

    Validate

    Checked missing values, duplicates, formats and clue counts across all rows; checked solution grids and givens on a 50,000-row sample.

  2. 02

    Test

    Measured Pearson and Spearman association across the complete 3M-row source.

  3. 03

    Engineer

    Added clue distribution, balance, location and rotational-symmetry features.

  4. 04

    Compare

    Evaluated clue-only and surface-feature models on an unseen 20,000-row test set.

What this supports

A catalogue-classification decision

Whether simple puzzle metadata predicts the supplied solver rating well enough for provisional labels.

What it does not prove

Human-perceived difficulty or product impact

The source rating comes from an automated solver, not observed player behaviour, enjoyment or retention.
