Can you judge a Sudoku by its clues?

Short answer

No, not reliably.

The numbers already filled in are called clues. Across these three million puzzles, their count tells us very little about the difficulty rated by a computer solver. Counting clues is a poor basis for calling a puzzle “Easy” or “Hard”.

The findings at a glance

One dashboard, one conclusion.

The dashboard shows why puzzles with the same number of starting clues can have very different computer difficulty ratings.

All three million puzzle records were analysed. The 2.2% figure means puzzles with a computer rating above 4.0. The charts show puzzles with 21 to 28 clues, covering 99.98% of the data.

What do the chart labels mean?

Correlation: a score showing how closely two measurements move together. The score of 0.035 is close to zero, so this check found almost no link between clue count and rating.

P90 − P10: the distance between the lower and upper ends of the middle 80% of ratings. It shows how much ratings differ while leaving out the lowest and highest 10%.

Scanning-only: the source computer solver finished the puzzle using its basic scanning method, without searching through possible answers. This does not mean every player would find it easy.

0.12% and 0.69%: how much of the difference in ratings the tested prediction rules explained. These are not percentages of correct answers.

The personal starting point

A player’s hunch became a product question.

After years of playing Sudoku, I had picked up enough techniques and tricks to know that fewer clues did not always mean a harder puzzle. I suspected that where the clues were placed mattered more than how many there were. But when friends found puzzles I considered relatively easy just as difficult as the others, I began to question what “difficulty” actually meant, and whether it came from the puzzle, the player or both.

A Sudoku app may need to label thousands, or even millions, of puzzles. If it uses a shortcut that does not match the effort needed to solve them, “Easy” and “Hard” become unreliable promises to the player.

What the analysis found

Three pieces of evidence changed the decision.

01

Clue count

More clues do not reliably tell us whether a puzzle is harder.

Two checks of the link between clue count and computer rating gave scores of 0.035 and 0.041. Both are close to zero, so neither check found a useful link. For this project, 0.10 was the minimum score set for further consideration, not a rule that applies to every analysis.

03

Prediction

The clue count and layout rules we tested predicted ratings poorly.

On puzzles not used to build the prediction rules, clue count explained just 0.12% of the differences in computer ratings. A separate rule combining clue count with basic measures of clue placement, balance and symmetry explained only 0.69%. Both did too poorly to recommend for difficulty labels.

The layout rule used simple straight-line relationships between basic grid measurements and ratings. We did not test every clue arrangement, solving technique or more complex prediction method.

My interpretation

What surprised me was not the clue count.

I expected clue count to tell us little about difficulty. What surprised me was how little the combination of clue count and our basic measures of clue placement, balance and symmetry explained: only 0.69% of the differences in computer ratings. It made me wonder whether difficulty depends more on the techniques and reasoning needed to solve a puzzle than on how it looks. That is a question for further study, not something this analysis proved.

That experience can differ from player to player. Familiarity with solving techniques, the amount of help needed and years of practice all shape which patterns someone can recognise. Sudoku now feels a little like chess to me: the more you play, the more you begin to see patterns that were invisible when you started.

Product recommendation

Let real player behaviour define difficulty.

Start with a computer estimate when a new puzzle has no player results yet. As people play, use their results to improve the label. Look at how long they take, how many hints and mistakes they make, and whether they restart or give up. Compare these results separately for beginners, intermediate players and advanced players, so the label fits their experience. If the app needs one label for everyone, combine the groups’ results in proportions that reflect how many players belong to each group.

This is my recommendation for the next stage. The current data contains no player results, so this approach has not been tested here.

How the conclusion was reached

How I checked the data and tested the idea.

I checked the data, tested the link with clue count, and tried two ways of predicting the computer ratings. The methods and their limits are explained below.

  1. 01

    Check

    Checked all three million records for missing information, duplicates, valid formats and correct clue counts. Checked completed grids and their starting clues for 50,000 puzzles.

  2. 02

    Test

    Used two checks to see how closely clue count follows the computer rating across all three million records.

  3. 03

    Measure

    For 100,000 puzzles, measured where clues sit, how evenly they are spread and whether their positions match when the grid is turned halfway around.

  4. 04

    Compare

    Built two prediction rules using 80,000 puzzles. Checked both on the same 20,000 puzzles that were kept out when building the rules.

What this supports

A reason to avoid clue count as the main label

In this dataset, clue count had almost no link with the computer rating, and the tested clue count and layout rules predicted it poorly.

What it does not prove

How hard real people find a puzzle

The ratings came from a computer solver. We did not measure how hard people found the puzzles, whether they enjoyed them or whether they kept playing. Results from this dataset may not hold for other puzzle collections.
