Can you judge a Sudoku by its clues?

Short answer

No—not reliably.

Clue count is almost unrelated to the supplied solver difficulty. A product using it as the main labelling rule would give players inconsistent expectations.

The personal starting point

A player’s hunch became a product question.

I had played Sudoku casually for months, and many patterns had become almost automatic. Watching friends struggle with puzzles that felt straightforward made me wonder what a difficulty label was actually measuring.

A digital Sudoku product may need to classify thousands—or millions—of boards. If it uses an intuitive shortcut that does not match solving effort, “Easy” and “Hard” become unreliable promises to the player.

The result that broke the shortcut 23 clues

Puzzles with exactly 23 clues range from a 0.0 solver rating to the dataset maximum of 8.5.

Same clue count. Entirely different solving experience.

What the analysis found

Three pieces of evidence changed the decision.

01

Association

More clues do not create a dependable difficulty ladder.

Pearson correlation between clue count and rating is 0.035; Spearman correlation is 0.041. Both sit well below the pre-stated 0.10 threshold for a useful relationship.

Within-group variation

Each common clue count still contains a wide spread.

For clue counts 21–28, the central 80% of ratings spans roughly three rating points. Counting givens does not tell a player what reasoning the puzzle will demand.

03

Prediction

Visible structure barely improves the prediction.

Adding clue balance, position and rotational symmetry raises test R² from 0.0012 to only 0.0069. The surface still does not contain enough signal.

Product recommendation

Stop using clue count as the primary difficulty label.

Use solver-based technique or effort measures to screen and provisionally tier puzzles. Before publishing those tiers, calibrate them against real player behaviour: completion time, hints, errors and abandonment, segmented by demonstrated skill.

Solver complexity→Provisional tier→Player validation

How the conclusion was reached

Built to withstand the obvious questions.

The analysis separates confirmatory tests from exploration and keeps the boundary between solver difficulty and human experience explicit.

  1. 01

    Validate

    Checked missing values, duplicates, grid length, clue counts and solution validity.

  2. 02

    Test

    Measured Pearson and Spearman association across the complete 3M-row source.

  3. 03

    Engineer

    Added clue distribution, balance, location and rotational-symmetry features.

  4. 04

    Compare

    Evaluated clue-only and surface-feature models on an unseen 20,000-row test set.

What this supports

A catalogue-classification decision

Whether simple puzzle metadata is sufficient for reliable difficulty labels.

What it does not prove

Human enjoyment or retention

The source rating comes from an automated solver, not observed player behaviour.

Source and reproducibility

The evidence is inspectable.
